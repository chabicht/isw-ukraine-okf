# Concept-Type Authority Doctrine

## Principle

Each OKF concept type owns a distinct layer of analytical content. A concept
should synthesize the content it owns into coherent, multi-author woven prose.
Cross-links connect concepts; they do not replace content. No concept should be
a stub farm — a sequence of one-line teasers pointing to another file.

## Ownership by type

### Actor (countries, leaders, organizations, authors)

Owns:

- Identity, structural characteristics, and posture
- Doctrine, worldview, and strategic culture
- Relationships (alliances, rivalries, dependencies)
- Domestic dynamics that shape external behavior
- Synthesis: "What kind of actor is this, and why does it behave this way?"

References out: conflict files for operational timelines, event files for
discrete occurrences, theme files for cross-cutting frameworks.

An actor concept may summarize an ongoing conflict in 2-3 sentences to establish
context, but the conflict's operations, escalation dynamics, and timeline belong
in the conflict concept. The actor owns the *posture* — the conflict owns the
*operations*.

### Conflict (wars, crises, sustained standoffs)

Owns:

- Operational and strategic narrative: timeline, military operations, diplomatic
  maneuvers, escalation dynamics
- The conflict's arc: how it began, how it evolved, where it stands
- Multi-author assessments of military outcomes, strategic logic, and failure
  modes
- Synthesis: "What happened, why, and what does it mean?"

References out: actor files for doctrine/identity, theme files for analytical
frameworks applied, event files for discrete turning points, region files for
geographic context.

A conflict concept may reference an actor's posture in a sentence, but the
actor's identity, doctrine, and structural characteristics belong in the actor
concept.

### Event (discrete occurrences)

Owns:

- The event's background and immediate dynamics
- Direct analytical implications of the event itself
- The event's own market, political, or strategic repricing
- Synthesis: "What happened on this occasion, and what does it change?"

References out: conflict file for broader context and ongoing dynamics, actor
files for the participants' postures.

An event concept must carry its own analytical weight. It is not a signpost to
the conflict file. If the event has a distinct analytical contribution (market
repricing, escalation trap analysis, framework application), that analysis
lives in the event file, not the conflict file.

### Theme (cross-cutting analytical topics and frameworks)

Owns:

- The framework itself: its concepts, indicators, mechanisms, and application
- The framework's evolution and validation against events
- Synthesis: "What is this analytical lens, how does it work, and what does it
  reveal?"

References out: conflict/event files for case material that illustrates the
framework, actor files for the actors the framework analyzes.

A theme concept owns its framework's content. The target-selection logic,
economic-attrition mechanism, and effectiveness indicators of Ukraine's
deep-strike campaign belong in the strike-campaign theme — not in the
Russia-Ukraine conflict file. The conflict file references the theme; it does
not absorb the theme's analytical content.

### Region (geographic areas with independent dynamics)

Owns:

- Regional dynamics: cross-actor patterns, geographic integration, shared
  infrastructure, regional institutions
- Synthesis: "What defines this region as a strategic space?"

References out: actor/conflict files for specific cases within the region.

Bundle-specific rule: this bundle's regions include the recurring ISW
operational axes (rules.md §7). An axis region owns sector-level operational
state and terrain-control detail — "who advanced where" on that axis — while
the conflict concept owns the war-level arc, cross-axis strategic narrative,
and escalation dynamics. Daily operational detail from ISW assessments
canonically homes on the axis region, not the conflict.

## Distribution principle

When the same topic is relevant to multiple concept types, each concept owns
the facet appropriate to its type. Ukraine's refinery strike campaign has a
conflict dimension (its role in the war's attrition dynamic), a theme dimension
(the strike campaign's economic model and indicators), an event dimension (a
specific major strike wave), and an actor dimension (Russia's fuel-supply
vulnerability and the Kremlin's response as posture). All four should exist;
none should absorb the others.

The test: a reader arriving at a concept should get a coherent, self-contained
answer to that concept type's synthesis question — not a list of stubs sending
them elsewhere for every substantive point.
