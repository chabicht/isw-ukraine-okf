# Current Situation

Russia's 2022 full-scale invasion of Ukraine fundamentally shifted and intensified the Russo-Iranian relationship. Tehran has leveraged Moscow's growing material and financial requirements to sustain its war effort and support Tehran's own domestic and foreign policy objectives. The relationship is anchored in a shared ideological interest in challenging and overturning the US-led world order, allowing it to weather tensions that have arisen since 2022.[1]

Russo-Iranian cooperation now operates along seven major axes spanning defense, economic, and political spheres. Moscow and Tehran seek different outcomes from their collaboration, but ISW assesses that the success of Russia cannot be separated from the success of Iran.[1]

Russian President Vladimir Putin and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian signed the Russia-Iran Comprehensive Strategic Partnership Agreement in Moscow on January 17, 2025. The agreement addresses enhanced defense cooperation, intelligence sharing, nuclear energy use and cooperation, transportation support and development of the International North-South Transport Corridor (INSTC), and a clause stipulating mutual prohibition on allowing third parties to use their territory to threaten the other's security. The agreement includes unspecified development of "military-technical cooperation" between the two countries.[2]

The bilateral agreement could lay the groundwork for Russia to establish aircraft refueling hubs and a naval presence in Iran, particularly as the fall of Bashar al-Assad's regime and waning Russian influence in the Middle East threatens Russian bases in Syria. Russia could use Iranian territory to support operations in North Africa and the Middle East, though Iran's geographic location is suboptimal compared to Syria's proximity to Libya and the Mediterranean. Russia may use the agreement to establish a more permanent military presence in Iran in the long term, but Iran may be reluctant due to potential additional Western sanctions and retaliation.[2]

The agreement notably lacks a mutual defense clause, indicating that Russia likely lacks the bandwidth to support significant operations outside of Ukraine and is prioritizing its manpower needs through its mutual defense treaty with North Korea. Russia appears to value its ability to mitigate manpower constraints through the North Korea mutual defense clause over further military-technical cooperation with Iran.[2]

Iran has supplied Russia with Shahed drones throughout the war. Ukrainian President Zelensky reported that Ukrainian forces shot down 7,800 Iranian Shahed drones in 2024 alone.[3]

# Analysis

### Defense Cooperation

Iran's provision of Shahed drones and assistance with domestic Russian production capabilities forms the core of defense cooperation. Potential Iranian ballistic missile transfers remain under discussion. The relationship provides Iran access to advanced Russian military technology and diplomatic cover.

### Economic and Sanctions Evasion

The INSTC and energy coordination offer Iran economic benefits while helping Russia bypass Western sanctions. Iran serves as a channel for Russian goods and potentially for sanctions evasion.
